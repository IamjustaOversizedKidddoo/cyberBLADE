# SOC L1 curriculum: claims log

Every claim in an L1 lesson that is not obviously certain gets a row here, so a human reviewer can check it before a lesson leaves `draft`. Confidence values: **certain** / **fairly sure** / **unsure**. Flashcards hold certain facts only.

| # | Claim | Lesson | Confidence | Where to verify |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | In many mail systems, `ESMTPSA` in a `Received:` line means the client authenticated (logged in) to submit the message. | soc-l1-email-headers-01 (worked example) | fairly sure | RFC 3848 (ESMTP transmission types: "ESMTPSA" = ESMTP with STARTTLS and SMTP AUTH). The wording is kept hedged ("in many mail systems") because servers are not required to record it. |
| 2 | The exact wording of `Received:` and `Authentication-Results:` lines varies by mail server product. | soc-l1-email-headers-01 | certain | RFC 5321 §4.4 (Received is loosely specified); RFC 8601 (Authentication-Results allows vendor-specific properties). |
| 3 | `Authentication-Results:` is trustworthy only when your own server added it; it starts with the server's own identifier (authserv-id). | soc-l1-email-headers-01 | certain | RFC 8601 §2.2 and §5 (receivers should remove or ignore look-alike headers claiming their authserv-id). |
| 4 | SPF `softfail` means the domain owner says the server is probably not allowed but asked receivers not to reject outright. | soc-l1-email-headers-01 (exercise explanation) | fairly sure | RFC 7208 §2.6.5. The receiver's actual handling of softfail is local policy, so the lesson does not claim what happens next. |
| 5 | DMARC passes when the visible `From:` domain aligns with a domain that passed SPF or DKIM; policies are none / quarantine / reject. | soc-l1-email-headers-01 | certain | RFC 7489 §3.1 (alignment) and §6.3 (p= tag). |
| 6 | With `p=none`, nothing is blocked automatically "so it reached the inbox". | soc-l1-email-headers-01 (worked example) | fairly sure | RFC 7489 §6.3. Real receivers can still filter on other signals; the example's world has no other filter. A reviewer may prefer "DMARC did not stop it". |
| 7 | Forwarding usually breaks SPF while a DKIM signature on unchanged content usually survives. | soc-l1-email-headers-01, checks q4, card 05 | certain (as a general rule) | RFC 7208 Appendix D (forwarding); RFC 6376 (DKIM signs content, independent of path). Mailing lists that modify content break DKIM too; the lesson says "some mailing lists break SPF" and does not claim DKIM always survives. |
| 8 | ARC (Authenticated Received Chain) preserves authentication results across forwarding. | soc-l1-email-headers-01 (In the real world) | certain | RFC 8617. Mentioned only in passing. |
| 9 | False positive vs benign true positive: "did the rule detect what it was designed to detect?" | soc-l1-triage-loop-01 | fairly sure | Industry usage differs; some teams use "benign positive" or fold both into "false positive". This lesson follows the disposition labels used by the platform's simulator (`content/soc/taxonomy.yaml`). The linter enforces that wording. |
| 10 | A reputation lookup returning "no data" is not evidence of safety. | soc-l1-triage-loop-01, card 03 | certain | Reasoning claim, not a source claim. |
| 11 | Many SOCs organise work in tiers (Tier 1 triage, Tier 2 investigation); some have no tiers. | soc-l1-soc-roles-01 | certain (as a generalisation) | Common industry practice; the lesson explicitly says teams vary. |
| 12 | Encoded PowerShell commands are often used by attackers, though some admin tools use them too. | soc-l1-soc-roles-01 (worked example) | certain | MITRE ATT&CK T1059.001 and T1027 (obfuscated commands); the `-EncodedCommand` parameter is documented by Microsoft for legitimate use. |
| 13 | Reputation services hold data about public addresses and domains, so an internal server name will almost never appear in one. | soc-l1-alert-lifecycle-01 | fairly sure | General behaviour of public reputation services; some commercial products index internal data you upload to them, which is why the lesson says "almost never". |
| 14 | Data protection laws in many countries (e.g. the EU's GDPR) limit how personal data is collected, used and kept; security monitoring is usually allowed for clear purposes. | soc-l1-data-handling-01 | fairly sure | GDPR Art. 5 (principles), Art. 6(1)(f) and Recital 49 (network and information security as a legitimate interest). The lesson states that it is not legal advice and that rules vary by country. |
| 15 | Public file-analysis services can share uploaded files and reports with the service and sometimes with other users. | soc-l1-data-handling-01 | fairly sure | Varies by service and plan; several well-known public services make submissions visible to other users or researchers. Generalised deliberately ("can", "sometimes"). |
| 16 | Some jurisdictions require works council agreement or specific notices before monitoring employees. | soc-l1-data-handling-01 (In the real world) | fairly sure | For example, co-determination rules in Germany (BetrVG §87(1) no. 6). Phrased as "some", without naming countries in the lesson. |
| 17 | IP geolocation is approximate (often country or region level) and reflects network registration or routing, not the person's location; VPNs and providers can place users elsewhere. | soc-l1-alert-anatomy-01, card 03 | fairly sure | Geolocation providers publish accuracy as varying by country and by fixed vs mobile networks; city-level results are often wrong. Kept general. |
| 18 | Attackers guessing passwords usually try many accounts or many more attempts than a user who forgot a password. | soc-l1-alert-anatomy-01 (worked example) | fairly sure | Typical of password spraying and brute force (ATT&CK T1110.001/.003). Lockout policies shape both patterns, so the lesson asks for one confirming check rather than relying on the pattern alone. |
| 19 | Whoever controls a domain controller can control every account in the domain; attackers often install services on hosts to keep access. | soc-l1-prioritisation-01 | certain | The role of domain controllers in Active Directory authentication; ATT&CK T1543.003 (Windows service persistence). |
| 20 | Some older ("legacy") sign-in protocols cannot perform multi-factor authentication, so a success through them can skip MFA. | soc-l1-dispositions-01 (worked example) | fairly sure | Basic-authentication mail protocols (e.g. IMAP, POP, older SMTP auth) do not support interactive MFA prompts; many providers have since disabled them. Kept generic ("an older sign-in method"). |
