> **Outcome:** you can work a large queue without losing the important alerts, use your time deliberately, and hand over a shift so nothing falls through the gap.

## Why this matters on shift

Some shifts start with two hundred alerts waiting. You cannot give each one ten minutes, and if you work them top to bottom the critical one at position 140 waits for hours. Then, at the end of the shift, everything you know has to reach the next analyst, or open problems quietly disappear overnight. Queue management and handover are where individual triage skill turns into a team that does not drop things.

## Core ideas

### First pass, then deep work

With a big queue, do a **first pass** before deep triage: a quick sweep through the whole queue to group, sort and spot the urgent alerts, spending seconds on each rather than minutes.

Think of a hospital emergency department on a busy night. A triage nurse sees everyone briefly first and sorts them. Only then do doctors start treating, in order of need.

During the first pass:

- **Group** alerts that share a rule, host or account, so you can work them together.
- **Sort** by priority: critical assets, privileged accounts, signs of success.
- **Flag** anything urgent to start at once, and tell the shift lead if there are more urgent alerts than you can handle.

### Working groups of similar alerts

Forty alerts with the same rule, host and pattern can often be handled together, but only after you check them. Open a sample, including the newest and the oldest, and look for anything different. Any alert that differs leaves the group and is triaged on its own. Never close a group from the title alone.

A **flapping rule** is a detection that fires, clears and fires again repeatedly because the activity hovers around its threshold. It floods the queue with near-identical alerts. Close them with a reason, and report the rule to detection engineering with examples.

### Using your time deliberately

- **Time-box** each alert. When you reach the limit without a decision, escalate or mark it "needs more information" with what is missing.
- **Say when the queue is beyond you.** Telling the shift lead early is professional; quietly falling behind is not.
- **Take short breaks.** Fatigue makes everything look the same, which is exactly how real alerts get missed.

### Handover

A **shift handover** is the structured transfer of open work, context and warnings from one shift to the next. A useful handover covers:

1. **Open incidents:** status, owner, next steps.
2. **Open alerts:** especially "needs more information" ones, with what you are waiting for and from whom.
3. **Watch items:** things that are not alerts yet but need an eye kept on them, such as a host that went quiet or a password reset you asked for.
4. **Noise and tooling:** flapping rules, broken sources, anything that will mislead the next shift.
5. **Anything promised:** updates owed to people, with times.

It should be written in the case or handover tool, and talked through when possible. The incoming analyst confirms they have it.

## Worked example

An analyst starts the morning shift at Larkspur Freight. The night shift's handover note says only: "Quiet night. Nothing major."

**A dead end.** The queue shows 162 open alerts. Reading the note, the analyst assumes they are routine and starts at the top. Twenty minutes later they find, by chance, an alert marked "needs more information: waiting for the server owner to confirm a new admin account on srv-hr03", opened at 01:40. Nobody had chased it, and the note did not mention it.

**The analyst restarts with a first pass.** Grouping shows 120 alerts from one rule that alternates on a backup server every few minutes (a flapping rule), 25 scanner alerts inside a change window, and 17 others. Within those 17 are the srv-hr03 admin account, still unconfirmed after six hours, and a sign-in success after many failures for a finance account at 05:12.

**What they do.** They chase the server owner and escalate the srv-hr03 account when there is still no answer, triage the 05:12 sign-in next, close the flapping and scanner alerts in checked groups with reasons, and report the flapping rule.

## Common mistakes

- **Working top to bottom without a first pass.** The urgent alert may be far down.
- **Closing groups by title.** Check a sample, including the newest and oldest.
- **Hiding a backlog.** Raise it early.
- **Handovers that say "all fine".** List open work, watch items and promises explicitly.
- **Leaving without confirmation** that the next analyst has the handover.

## What good looks like

A strong analyst starts with the incoming handover, does a quick first pass, works in priority order with time boxes, closes groups only after checking samples, raises workload problems early, and ends with a structured handover the next analyst confirms.

## In the real world

Most SOCs have a handover template and a short overlap between shifts. Shift leads watch queue age and volume and move people between queues when one spikes. Flapping rules and noisy sources are a major cause of alert fatigue, which is why good teams track and fix them rather than living with them.

The simulator's alert-storm shift is deliberately high-volume and noisy, with repeating rules and scanner bursts, so it is the right place to practise a first pass.

## Remember it like this

First pass, priority order, time boxes, and a handover with open work, watch items, noise and promises.

## Interview answer

"With a big queue I do a quick first pass: group similar alerts, sort by priority and flag anything urgent, and I tell the shift lead early if it's more than I can handle. I time-box each alert, and I only close groups of similar alerts after checking a sample. At the end of the shift I write a structured handover: open incidents, open alerts with what they're waiting for, watch items, noisy rules or broken sources, and anything I've promised, and I confirm the next analyst has it."
