> **Outcome:** you can detect password attacks by counting the right things, recognise attacks designed to stay under lockout limits, and decide what to do when one succeeds.

## Why this matters on shift

Stolen and guessed passwords are among the most common ways attackers get in. The attacks that work are rarely the loud ones that lock accounts. They are quiet: one password per account per hour, spread across many addresses, or a list of real passwords leaked from another company's breach. Catching them means counting cleverly, not just counting failures.

## Core ideas

### Three kinds of password attack

You met brute force (many passwords, one account) and password spraying (few passwords, many accounts). A third is just as important:

**Credential stuffing** (ATT&CK T1110.004) means trying user name and password pairs leaked from other websites' breaches, betting that people reuse passwords. Each account is usually tried once, with a password the person really used somewhere. That gives credential stuffing a much higher success rate than guessing.

Think of three burglars. One tries every key on a huge ring in a single door (brute force). One tries the most common key in every door on the street (spraying). One has a bag of real keys copied from people's other homes, and tries each in the matching house (stuffing).

### Low and slow

A **low-and-slow attack** is spread out in time and across sources so that no single counter crosses an alert or lockout threshold. Examples: one attempt per account every few hours, or attempts from hundreds of different addresses, each making only one or two tries. Lockout policies and simple "10 failures in 5 minutes" rules do not see them.

### Count the right things

**Distinct count** means counting how many *different* values appear, such as how many different user names one address tried. It is the key to spotting the quiet attacks:

| Question | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| Failures per account | Brute force on one account |
| Distinct accounts per source | Spraying or stuffing from one source |
| Distinct sources per account | Distributed attacks on one account |
| Failures for unknown user names | Name guessing |
| Successes from sources that mostly failed | The attempt that worked |

The query drills linked below practise two of these: counting sign-ins by outcome, and counting distinct users.

### When one succeeds

A success from an attacking source means the attacker is using a real account (ATT&CK T1078, Valid Accounts). After that, look for:

- multi-factor prompts the user did not expect, or a sign-in that skipped MFA,
- sign-ins from a new device or location,
- what the account did next: mailbox rules, file access, admin changes.

And widen the search: any other account with a success from the same sources.

## Worked example

Larkspur Freight's identity provider raises an alert: "Many failed sign-ins from 172.16.40.250". The analyst counts:

<LogBlock
  title="Failed sign-ins, last 24 hours (synthetic)"
  lines={[
    "source 172.16.40.250   2,310 failures   410 distinct accounts   mostly wrong password",
    "sources 192.0.2.10 - 192.0.2.39 (30 addresses)   61 failures   58 distinct accounts   each source 1-3 tries",
  ]}
/>

**The false lead.** 410 accounts failing from one address looks like a huge spray. But 172.16.40.250 is the company's email web gateway, which signs users in on their behalf. Every user's sign-in appears to come from it. The failures are spread across the day in office hours, the success rate is normal, and the count of distinct accounts simply matches the number of staff using web mail. This is ordinary traffic seen through one shared address.

**The quiet attack.** Thirty outside addresses each made only one to three attempts. No single source crossed any threshold, and no account was locked. But together they tried 58 different accounts, all with the same pattern: one attempt, a pause of about an hour, then another account. Grouping by time instead of by source shows the coordination. The analyst then searches for successes from those 30 addresses:

<LogBlock
  title="Successes from 192.0.2.10 - 192.0.2.39 (synthetic)"
  lines={[
    "03:14  success  account t.mbeki  source 192.0.2.27  mfa=not_prompted (trusted location rule)",
  ]}
/>

One account was signed in, and MFA was not asked for because of a misconfigured "trusted location" rule. The analyst escalates t.mbeki as compromised, lists the 30 addresses, and flags the trusted-location rule.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting only failures.** Distinct accounts and distinct sources reveal the quiet attacks.
- **Assuming no lockouts means no attack.** Low-and-slow attacks are designed to avoid lockouts.
- **Blaming shared addresses.** Gateways, proxies and VPNs put many users behind one address.
- **Stopping at "attack detected".** The search for successes is the most important step.
- **Forgetting other accounts.** Search every success from the attacking sources.

## What good looks like

A strong analyst groups and counts in several directions, recognises shared infrastructure, finds coordinated low-and-slow attempts, always searches for successes, and escalates with the full list of sources and affected accounts.

## In the real world

Identity providers and SIEMs offer built-in detections for spraying and credential stuffing, and many add risk scores for unfamiliar locations or known-bad addresses. Attackers often use residential proxy networks, which make each attempt come from a different ordinary-looking home address. Strong defences combine MFA, checks against known-leaked passwords and sensible lockout rules.

## Remember it like this

Count different names and different addresses, not just failures, then find the one that got in.

## Interview answer

"Brute force is many passwords on one account, spraying is a few passwords across many accounts, and credential stuffing uses leaked pairs from other breaches, so it has a high success rate. Quiet attacks spread attempts over time and sources to stay under lockout limits, so I count distinct accounts per source and distinct sources per account, and watch out for shared addresses like gateways. Most importantly I search for any success from the attacking sources, check MFA and what the account did next, and widen to other accounts."
